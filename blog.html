Workshop 4: Data Visualization

This week, as an extension of data practice, we shared our observation about the homework — collect and code data that related to our selected topic. Besides, we also learned about how to visualize data that we have already collected.

During the workshop, we shared our current difficulties about data collecting and the measures to improve if we have enough time. We mentioned that the data we had collected may be subjective, because it is hard to put a post into an exact category — they often lie somewhere between categories. We also mentioned that if we have enough, we may compare data from different platforms and different period.

What I learned from this workshop are as follows. First, there are lots of ways to visualize data, such as excel, tableau and some other tools. The charts that we made have to be easy to read, they have to have a title, have to tell the audience where the data comes from. Second, different types of data need to be presented with chart types suited to them. The data sample we collected this time was relatively small, and most of the data were about frequency rather than showing trends over time. We therefore chose bar charts and pie charts to visualise the data.

Perhaps because I had some prior exposure to data visualisation and a little experience with chart-making, I found this session relatively manageable for me. All in all, I consider that with the more workshop that I took, the better I adjust with the pace and the content.


Workshop 5: Identity, Algorithmic Identity, and Data

In workshop 5, we discussed how algorithms construct our algorithmic identity, and does the algorithmic identity really lead to accurate representation of ourselves?

During the workshop, we discuss about the data that we shared on social media. At first, our group chose to analyse data in Instagram, but as we couldn’t find the page about algorithms, we considered it might be the version we used has some problems or difficulties. So we change the platform to rednote. It showed that I am a person whose interests are wide-raged, I concern about daily life and celebrity news, and I also like to follow overseas education. As Cheney-Lippold argues, the algorithmic identity is dynamic and fluid, but it contains stable elements based on our repeated behaviors. Like my long-term habit of watching vloggers made platform give me “daily life” tag, but my recent search about overseas study posts immediately made platform updated my preference by adding “overseas education” tag.

After that, we tried to collect data from 32 of our friends on the platform and chose the latest 15 posts from each person. What stuck me in this process are as follows. First of all, it showed that we don’t have 32 friends on one social media except WeChat. But then Holly told us that we can also collect data from people who we followed. Second, the boundary between two catagories is really hard to be clear. For example, sometimes my friend posts her travel with her friends, should I put this post into “outdoors” or “friends” or “lifestyle”? I consider it should be considered and considered again, and maybe I can try to find the intent of the post or just ask for my friend’s feedbacks. In this regard, I am certain that the algorithmic tags assigned to me are not entirely accurate. Additionally, I found it was sometimes difficult to find posts from friends that rarely post at all. So I consider the that part of missing data may made my overall dataset less accurate and complete.

All in all, after this workshop, I will be more cautious when considering algorithmic assessment and labelling of people. Every person is complicated and unique, we can’t truly understand a person genuinely through algorithms alone.

Reference
